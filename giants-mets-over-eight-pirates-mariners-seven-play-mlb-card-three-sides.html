How The Units Are Counted

The Pirates at -185 need 64.91 percent, which is the highest bar on the card by seven and a half points. The Mariners at -133 need 57.08 and the Brewers at -134 need 57.26. The Tigers and Guardians under at -115 needs 53.49, the two -110 tickets need 52.38, and the Giants and Mets over at even money needs exactly 50. The whole card, 12.845 divided by 22.845, is 56.23 percent.

The distribution is where the day actually lives. Pittsburgh is 21.60 percent of the risk, Seattle 20.71, Milwaukee 15.65, and the four totals are 12.85, 11.68, 8.95 and 8.56. The three sides are 7.445 units of the 12.845. If all three lose, the four totals winning outright still leaves the day down. That is the honest exposure and it is the opposite of the flat card from yesterday.

A Bullpen Day At Citi Field

Cesar Perdomo is the listed San Francisco starter and he has zero career starts. Four innings on the season. A 2.25 ERA across those four innings, which is not a number anybody should read as information. This is a bullpen day that has been announced in advance, and it is the reason there is an over on the board at all.

Christian Scott is the Mets counter at 3.80 over 94.2 innings across 20 starts with a 1.31 WHIP. Fine, not dominant, and he is only half of the game anyway.

The two run prevention numbers do the rest. San Francisco is 59-84 with a 4.39 staff ERA allowing 4.71 runs a game, the worst on this card. New York is 64-78 allowing 4.52 on a 4.13. Add those and you are at 9.23 before adjusting anything for the fact that one side has no defined starter. Eight is too low.

The case against is that neither lineup is good. The Giants score 4.20 a game and the Mets 4.17, both below the league middle. This is an over built on pitching that leaks rather than hitting that produces, and those get there in the sixth and seventh innings rather than the first.

The Price On The Pirates

The most expensive ticket on the card belongs to the game where the underdog has the better starter ERA, and that deserves saying plainly rather than burying.

Walbert Ureña is at 2.85 over 123.1 innings with opponents hitting .203 off him. Paul Skenes is at 3.97 over 149.2. On those two lines alone the Angels should be favoured.

The number hiding underneath is 64 walks in 123.1 innings. Ureña carries a 1.26 WHIP built almost entirely out of free passes, which is a profile that survives against lineups that swing at everything and stops surviving the moment somebody makes him throw strikes. Skenes has 179 strikeouts against 44 walks and a 1.16 WHIP.

Then the lineups. Los Angeles is 54-88 and scores 4.06 runs a game on a .678 OPS, the weakest offense playing today. Pittsburgh is 70-73 but scores 4.85 on a .733 OPS and is at home. The -185 is a price on the two lineups and the two bullpens, not on the two ERAs, and at 64.91 percent break even it needs to be right nearly two times in three.

Why The Whole Number Instead Of -1.5

Seattle -1 is not a rounding of -1.5. It is a different bet, and in 2026 the difference is worth measuring rather than assuming.

Across 2,138 completed regular season games this year, 583 finished by exactly one run. That is 27.27 percent, and it is the most common margin in baseball by a wide distance. Every one of those games is a loss at -1.5 and a refund at -1. Seattle specifically wins 31.8 percent of its games by exactly one run, well above the league share, and home teams stop batting the moment they take a ninth inning lead, which manufactures one run wins that would have been three run wins on the road.

The line was not the same everywhere this morning. One book had Seattle -1 at -145 and another had it at -133 at the same minute. The card takes -133.

Underneath it, Oakland is 57-86 with a 5.42 staff ERA and 5.71 runs allowed a game, the worst run prevention on this slate. Gage Jump is 5.15 over 92.2 innings with a 1.49 WHIP and 45 walks. Bryan Woo has a 1.09 WHIP over 148.1 innings with 149 strikeouts, and his 4.25 ERA is a home run problem rather than a traffic problem.

The honest worry is the Seattle offense. The Mariners score 3.97 runs a game, the lowest figure on this entire card. Winning by two requires scoring, and scoring is the thing Seattle has been worst at all year.

The First Pitch Of The Day

Milwaukee at Cincinnati, 12:10 pm eastern, and it is the simplest read on the board. The Brewers are 88-55, the best record in baseball, scoring 5.02 runs a game on a .739 OPS with a 3.51 staff ERA allowing 3.82. No other club playing today is top of the league at both ends.

Cincinnati is 68-74, scoring 4.18 on a .706 OPS, with a 4.66 staff ERA allowing 4.94 a game. Brady Singer has allowed 26 home runs in 141.2 innings with a 1.45 WHIP and opponents hitting .281. Twenty six home runs in a park that gives them up is most of the argument. Kyle Harrison counters at 3.45 over 107.0 innings with a 1.20 WHIP and 130 strikeouts.

At -134 this is a fair price rather than a bargain. The Reds win 35.3 percent of their games by exactly one run, the highest share in baseball, which is a polite way of saying they hang around.

Eleven At Altitude

Eleven is a loud number and it only exists because of the building, so the only question is whether it is high enough.

The two run prevention figures answer most of it. Colorado allows 5.70 runs a game on a 5.44 staff ERA and St. Louis allows 4.60 on a 4.29. That is 10.30 combined before anybody adjusts for altitude, and half of Colorado's number was already earned at altitude.

Tanner Gordon has given up 17 home runs in 88.2 innings with opponents hitting .301, the worst opponent average of any starter on this card. Colorado is 55-87 and still posts a .740 OPS, the highest team OPS playing today, because Coors does that to a batting line.

The case against is Kyle Leahy, who has been genuinely good at 3.13 over 129.1 innings. His 1.31 WHIP says he allows runners, and runners at altitude is exactly the mechanism that clears an eleven. One unit, because it is the biggest number on the card and big numbers deserve small tickets.

The Two Unders

Tigers and Guardians under 7 is Gavin Williams. Twenty eight starts, 3.81 over 163.0 innings, a 1.09 WHIP and 217 strikeouts with opponents hitting .213. The ERA is inflated by 27 home runs and the rest of the line is far better than the number. Cleveland is 72-71 and scores 4.03 a game on a .685 OPS, the second weakest offense on the card. The risk is Detroit's Jackson Jobe, who has five starts and 23.1 innings at 4.63 with a 1.46 WHIP, so a short outing puts the bullpen in early. What protects it is that Detroit's bullpen is real, a 3.69 staff ERA allowing 4.04.

Yankees and Padres under 7 is the cleanest one on the card. Gerrit Cole at 3.29 over 104.0 innings with a 1.11 WHIP, Michael King at 3.11 over 162.0 with a 1.17, at Petco Park. New York carries the best staff ERA in this whole group at 3.22 and allows 3.70 runs a game, the lowest figure anywhere on the slate. San Diego allows 4.16 on a 3.92. Take the season scoring rates at face value, 4.53 and 4.21, and you get 8.74, which is exactly why it is 1.5 units and not three. The park and the two arms pull it under, not the lineups.

Bottom Line

Ten units to win against 12.845 at risk, needing 56.23 percent. The Giants and Mets over at +100 is the best price on the card relative to what the number should be, because one side has no starter and both sides leak runs. The Pirates at -185 is the most expensive ticket and the one where the underdog has the better ERA, which is a real argument and not a small one. The Mariners -1 is the play where the line shopping mattered more than the handicapping.

If this day goes wrong, it goes wrong on the three sides. Four totals cannot carry a card where the sides are 58 percent of the money.
